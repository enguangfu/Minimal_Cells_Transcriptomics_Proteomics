\subsection{Correlation between Transcriptomics and Proteomics in the Reduced Organism}

% \textbf{Excel Sheet \newline\textit{Gene-level Transcription and Translation Strength}}

Absolute protein abundances were derived from the \syn~mass-spectrometry proteome by converting each protein's intensity-based absolute quantification (iBAQ) value to a relative abundance in parts per million (iPM), and then to an intracellular copy number using the protein content per cell (Methods).
Of the 828 annotated proteins, 721 (87\%) were detected.
Copy number was strongly localization-dependent: cytoplasmic proteins had a median of 47 copies per cell ($n = 516$), compared with 21 for lipoproteins ($n = 68$), 10 for membrane proteins ($n = 126$), and 3 for extracellular proteins ($n = 11$), and the most abundant protein was the translation elongation factor Tu (\gene{tuf}{0151}) at roughly 7{,}200 copies (Fig.~\ref{fig:correlation}a).
The low membrane and secreted values reflect the poor recovery of these classes by trypsin-only digestion rather than genuinely low abundance.

Two orthogonal transcriptome measurements were available for \syn, short-read Illumina and long-read PacBio.
Their per-gene sense transcripts per million (TPM) agreed moderately, with a Pearson correlation of $r = 0.62$ on $\log_{10}$ TPM ($n = 884$) and no systematic abundance- or length-dependent bias between the platforms (Fig.~\ref{fig:correlation}c).
Illumina sense TPM, which correlated more strongly with the proteome, was therefore adopted as the transcriptome axis for all subsequent correlations.

Across the detected proteome, $\log_{10}$ iPM and $\log_{10}$ Illumina TPM were positively correlated (Pearson $r = 0.61$, $R^{2} = 0.38$, $n = 717$).
Restricting the comparison to cytoplasmic proteins, which are quantified most reliably, raised the correlation to $r = 0.70$ ($R^{2} = 0.49$, $n = 512$; Fig.~\ref{fig:correlation}b).
Transcript abundance is thus the primary determinant of protein abundance in \syn, with roughly half of the variance left to post-transcriptional effects.

To dissect this residual variance, defined as the offset of each protein's $\log_{10}$ iPM from the transcript fit, three post-transcriptional layers were tested in turn.
Translation-initiation rates (TIR) predicted for every coding sequence with OSTIR~\cite{roots_ostir_2021} were largely uninformative: $\log_{10}$(TIR) correlated only weakly with the residual (Pearson $r = 0.17$), and adding it to the model raised $R^{2}$ by just 0.020, about 2\% of the variance (Fig.~\ref{fig:correlation}d).
This most likely reflects the limited accuracy of de novo initiation-rate prediction rather than an absence of initiation control.

The codon adaptation index (CAI)~\cite{sharp_codon_1987}, a proxy for translation-elongation efficiency, was by contrast a substantial predictor: it correlated with the residual (Pearson $r = 0.36$) and increased $R^{2}$ by 0.080, a 21\% gain over the transcript-only baseline (Fig.~\ref{fig:correlation}e,f).
Elongation efficiency, set by codon usage against the AT-rich genome, therefore measurably tunes protein output beyond transcript level.

Protein turnover, the third layer, has not been measured in \syn, so intrinsic half-lives were transferred from the related minimal bacterium~\mpn~\cite{burgos_protein_2020} by reciprocal-best-hit orthology and scaled by protease abundance (Methods).
The 245 mapped half-lives were long, with a median of 32~h and a minimum of 4.7~h, far exceeding the roughly 1~h doubling time of \syn (Fig.~\ref{fig:correlation}g).
Half-life was uncorrelated with the residual (Pearson $r = -0.12$, $P = 0.06$, $n = 242$) and added at most 0.01 to $R^{2}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:correlation}h).
Intrinsic degradation therefore has little influence on steady-state protein abundance in this fast-growing cell, where turnover is dominated by dilution through growth.
