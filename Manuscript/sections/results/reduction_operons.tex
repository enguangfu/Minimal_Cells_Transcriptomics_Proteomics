\subsection{Genome minimization remodels operon structure rather than the retained sequence}

\jsyna~(\syna) was derived from \jsyn~(\syn) by removing genes judged non-essential for growth in rich medium, shrinking the genome by roughly half~\cite{hutchison_design_2016}.
To ask how this minimization reshaped transcription, the \syna~genome was aligned back onto \syn, recasting the reduction as a set of discrete, locatable deletions on a single coordinate frame (Methods).
The genome contracted from 1{,}078{,}809 to 543{,}379~bp through 95 deletions ($\geq 50$~bp) that removed $\sim$536~kb (536{,}543~bp), with a mean deletion of 5{,}647~bp and a largest single cut of 71{,}578~bp.
Across the retained backbone the two genomes were 99.90\% identical, differing by only 36 single-nucleotide polymorphisms and 12 short indels, with no inversions, no translocations, and gene order preserved apart from one relocated gene (\gene{lap}{0154}).
Minimization was therefore a structural campaign of excision rather than a sequence-level rewiring, so expression differences at retained genes can be attributed to changes in transcriptional context rather than to sequence divergence (Fig.~\ref{fig:reduction_operons}a).

Overlaying the 95 deletions onto the 459 \syn~operons at single-base resolution showed that whole-operon excision dominated over partial truncation (Methods).
At the gene level 235 of the 459 operons (51.2\%) lost every sense gene, while 172 (37.5\%) kept all of theirs intact, leaving only 52 operons partially deleted (21 truncated at the leading edge, 20 at the lagging edge, and 11 internally).
The span-level classification agreed: 181 operons were fully deleted and 162 were untouched, with the remaining boundary truncations cutting into a coding sequence more often at the \threeprime\ end (47 operons) than at the \fiveprime\ end (28 operons).
In total 414 of the 911 \syn~genes were overlapped by a deletion.
The reduction thus preferentially removed entire operons, and it is the minority of operons left partially truncated that create the regulatory effects examined next.

Each deletion was then reframed as a junction between the nearest \syna-retained operon on either side, whose relative orientation determines whether a new co-transcribed unit can form (Methods).
Of the 95 junctions, 53 were tandem (the two operons co-oriented, the only configuration in which one transcript can run across the junction), 19 convergent, 15 divergent, and 8 internal to a single operon.
Among the tandem junctions the loss of the facing terminator and promoter defined four outcomes, of which clean excision of whole operon(s) between two intact neighbours was by far the most common (30 junctions), followed by read-through extension (11), decapitation (9), and true fusion of both flanking operons into one chimeric unit (only 3).
Testing the newly adjacent cross-junction gene pair against \syna~reads confirmed this hierarchy: fusion junctions were co-transcribed in 2 of 3 cases (67\%), against only 3 of 30 clean-excision controls (10\%) and a pristine-operon baseline of 27 of 45 (60\%).
The clearest fusion (DEL\_014) joined operons OP\_00043 and OP\_00050 across a deleted span, placing \gene{rpsT}{0082} downstream of \locus{0094} in one transcription unit supported by 37 bridging reads, a pairing absent in \syn, where \locus{0082} and \locus{0083} are co-transcribed instead (Fig.~\ref{fig:reduction_operons}b,c).
The new unit nonetheless transcribed \gene{rpsT}{0082} only weakly (fold change 0.074), because the fused upstream promoter of \locus{0094} is itself weak, so structural fusion did not restore the lost expression.
A second ribosomal-protein gene, \gene{rpsO}{0294} (S15), followed the same route, losing its original promoter to a deletion and acquiring a weak fused replacement, and likewise collapsed in transcription (fold change 0.144).
New operon fusion is therefore real but rare, and where it does occur the fused promoter is too weak to sustain the downstream gene, so the dominant junction outcome remains the clean removal of whole operons between neighbours that each keep their own regulators.

To resolve the consequence for individual retained genes, every \syn~gene kept in \syna~was classified by how the reduction changed the source of its transcription (Methods).
Most retained genes were structurally unaffected (360) or lost only downstream context (45), whereas 42 genes belonged to operons whose own promoter was deleted with no replacement (\texttt{promoter\_lost}), and smaller groups gained read-in from an upstream operon that lost its terminator (\texttt{readthrough\_exposed}, 24), shifted in promoter proximity (17), were disconnected from a retained promoter by an internal split (6), or acquired a new upstream promoter by fusion (3).
Comparing \syna~transcript fold changes across these classes isolated promoter loss as the single class that robustly dropped in expression, with a median fold change of 0.44 against 0.76 for unaffected genes (two-sided Mann--Whitney $p = 2.7 \times 10^{-4}$), while no other class differed significantly (Fig.~\ref{fig:reduction_operons}d).
The DNA-binding protein \gene{hupA}{0350} is the clearest case: its promoter lies within the neighbouring gene \locus{0349}, which the reduction deleted, so the operon was decapitated and \gene{hupA}{0350} collapsed from a relative transcript abundance of 6.68 to 0.13 (fold change 0.020) and a relative protein abundance of 6.48 to 0.092 (fold change 0.014) (Fig.~\ref{fig:reduction_operons}e).
Scanning the deleted segment with the same $-10$ detection applied to the canonical operons (Methods) recovered a perfect \texttt{TANAAT} hexamer (\texttt{TATAAT}) at the $-10$ position, backed by the extended \texttt{TNNTANAAT} motif, confirming that minimization removed a genuine strong promoter rather than incidental sequence.
Promoter-source loss, whether by outright deletion as in \gene{hupA}{0350} or by replacement with a weaker fused promoter as in the ribosomal-protein genes above, rather than any change in the retained sequence, is thus the structural event that most strongly suppresses a retained gene.
