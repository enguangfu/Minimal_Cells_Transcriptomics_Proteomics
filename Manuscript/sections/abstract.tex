\section{Abstract}

\jsyn~(\syn) and \jsyna~(\syna), genetically synthetic and genome-reduced versions of the naturally occurring bacterium \Mmy, are landmark platforms for defining the gene set required for life, yet how their genomes are expressed at the RNA level remains uncharted.
We combined full-length long-read RNA sequencing, short-read quantification, and complementary proteomics to map transcription, RNA processing, and translation across both cells.
Long reads resolved 459 operons (911 genes) in \syn~with matched promoters and terminators, whose RNA isoforms were pervasively reshaped by 3$'$-biased processing.
Transcript level was the strongest single determinant of protein abundance in both synthetic bacteria, and the antisense and intergenic transcription complicating \syn~traced to inherited mis-annotation and synthetic artifacts that minimization preferentially removed in \syna.
Halving the genome to \syna~unexpectedly suppressed several key genes by deleting their promoters, collapsing the nucleoid protein HupA and lowering central-carbon enzymes, and reallocated a third of the coding mRNA pool onto a single 21-gene ribosomal-protein operon.
RNA polymerase and central-metabolism enzymes fell while the RNase~Y degradosome rose, at both the RNA and protein level.
Together these shifts as the synthetic evolution suggest plausible mechanisms for \syna's reduced chromosome contacts and slower division.
These data provide the RNA-level foundation for whole-cell modeling of the minimal cell.
