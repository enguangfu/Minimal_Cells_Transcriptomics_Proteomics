\section{Introduction}

Bacterial genes are transcribed in operons and their transcripts are further shaped by ribonucleolytic processing, organizing the transcriptome above the level of the single gene~\cite{jacob_genetic_1961}.
The synthetic bacteria \jsyn~(\syn) and its minimized derivative \jsyna~(\syna) are landmark platforms for defining the minimal requirements of cellular life~\cite{gibson_creation_2010, hutchison_design_2016}, yet they have been characterized chiefly through genome design and protein essentiality~\cite{breuer_essential_2019}, leaving how their genes are organized and processed at the RNA level largely uncharted.

Three questions follow.
How is gene expression organized across a synthetic genome, from operon boundaries and transcription signals to downstream RNA processing?
Does transcription predict the proteome once the genetic background is stripped toward minimal complexity?
And what does genome minimization itself do to the expression program, beyond deleting genes?

We combined full-length long-read RNA sequencing (PacBio for \syn, Oxford Nanopore for \syna), short-read quantification, and matched proteomics to build a genome-wide, operon-resolved map of transcription, RNA processing, and translation for both organisms, and to compare how halving the genome reshaped it.
This atlas supplies the RNA-level foundation that whole-cell modeling of the minimal cell has so far lacked~\cite{thornburg_fundamental_2022}.
