Genetically minimized bacteria provide a unique opportunity to elucidate the inherent phenomena governing bacterial life \citep{lachance_minimal_2019}.~\jsyn~(\syn, GenBank CP002027.1) and its minimized derivative \jsyna~(\syna, GenBank CP016816.2) are genetically modified and reduced versions of the naturally occurring bacterium \Mmy~\cite{gibson_creation_2010, hutchison_design_2016} and they are landmark platforms for defining the minimal requirements of cellular life.
In synthesizing the \syn~genome (1{,}078{,}809~bp, 911 genes), 14 wild-type \mmy~genes were either deleted or disrupted and five non-native genes required for the cloning procedure in yeast as well as four watermarks were incorporated~\cite{gibson_creation_2010}.
Genome reduction resulted in \synthreezero, which to date has the least number of genes in any bacteria~\cite{hutchison_design_2016}.
However, its inconsistent morphology motivated the re-introduction of 19 genes resulting in more robust \syna~with normal cell division~\cite{pelletier_genetic_2021, thornburg_bringing_2026}.
Reducing the genome from \syn~to \syna~(543{,}379~bp, 493 genes), while preserving the ancestral order of the retained genes, produced the minimal genome of an autonomously replicating organism.
\syna~in particular has drawn interest from laboratories worldwide seeking the essence of life, through studies of gene essentiality and metabolism~\cite{breuer_essential_2019}, three-dimensional organization~\cite{gilbert_generating_2021}, and cell division~\cite{pelletier_genetic_2021}.
Together these efforts enabled the first whole-cell model resolved in three-dimensional space across an entire cell cycle~\cite{thornburg_fundamental_2022, thornburg_bringing_2026}.

The transcriptional program of both \syn~and \syna~is correspondingly spare, which makes the underlying regulation easier to resolve.
\Mycs~are themselves a product of natural genome reduction, having descended from low-G+C Gram-positive ancestors related to \Bsub~through massive gene loss as obligate parasites of stable host niches~\cite{razin_molecular_1998}.
In shedding much of the machinery that free-living bacteria use to cope with changing environments, \syn~retains only a single sigma factor and a small set of transcriptional regulators (18 genes), alongside a handful of housekeeping non-coding RNAs (Table~\ref{tbl:regulators}).
\syna~carries the reduction further, but by design rather than by evolution: its genome was engineered to keep only the genes needed for growth in a constant laboratory medium~\cite{hutchison_design_2016, breuer_essential_2019}, and it discards 6 of the prior 18 regulators while keeping the same lone sigma factor.
With few dedicated regulators and minimal regulatory RNA, \syn~and \syna~expose the core, largely constitutive transcription shared by all bacteria with little confounding control.

Bacterial genes are co-transcribed in operons~\cite{jacob_genetic_1961}, with multiple transcription start and termination sites (TSS and TTS) acting across a single gene cluster, and downstream endo- and exoribonucleolytic processing further generates a spectrum of overlapping and truncated RNA isoforms (Fig.~\ref{fig:operon}a).
Resolving operons requires reading transcripts at full length: short-read Illumina sequencing quantifies transcript abundance robustly but fragments RNA and cannot show which genes share a transcript~\cite{stark_rna_2019}, whereas long-read sequencing PacBio and Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) recovers full-length molecules and so exposes operon co-transcription, processing isoforms, and antisense or intergenic transcription~\cite{yan_smrt-cappable-seq_2018, grunberger_nanopore_2022}. A recent custom full-length RNA sequencing protocol SEnd-seq revealed unexpected bidirectional transcription terminations~\cite{ju_full-length_2019} . See \hyperref[method:rnaseq]{Methods} for detailed comparison between long-read and short-read RNA sequencing platforms.

Despite the importance of operonal structures in bacterial gene expression, both \syn~and \syna~have so far been defined chiefly at the level of genome design and protein essentiality~\cite{breuer_essential_2019}, leaving how their genes are organized and processed at the RNA level largely uncharted.
The two also differ in phenotype: \syna~divides more slowly in 2 hours instead of 1 hour of \syn~\cite{pelletier_genetic_2021}, packs a denser cytosol~\cite{gilbert_generating_2021}, and lacks persistent chromosome supercoiling, the last linked to its collapsed level of the nucleoid-associated protein HupA~\cite{gilbert_generating_2021}.
Here, we address three questions that have emerged in the gene expression of \syn~and \syna~as a result of their characterization to date.
First, how is gene expression organized across a synthetic genome, from operon boundaries and transcription signals to downstream RNA processing?
Second, beyond the deletions themselves, what does this minimization, as a form of synthetic evolution, do to the transcription and translation of the retained genes?
And finally, how does that minimization contribute to the phenotypic differences between the two cells? 
We quantitatively address these questions by capturing full-length transcripts with PacBio and ONT in \syn~and ONT in \syna, using Illumina short reads as the standard for transcript quantification, and combined these with proteomics to build a genome-wide, operon-resolved map of transcription, RNA processing, and translation in both cells.
This atlas supplies the RNA-level foundation of gene co-expression and quantification that whole-cell modeling of the minimal cell has so far lacked.
