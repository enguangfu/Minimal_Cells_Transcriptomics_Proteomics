\section{Introduction}

\jsyn~(\syn, GenBank CP002027.1) and its minimized derivative \jsyna~(\syna, GenBank CP016816.2), genetically modified and reduced versions of the naturally occurring bacterium \Mmy~\cite{gibson_creation_2010, hutchison_design_2016}, are landmark platforms for defining the minimal requirements of cellular life.
In synthesizing the \syn~genome (1{,}078{,}809~bp, 911 genes), 14 wild-type \mmy~genes were either deleted or disrupted and five non-native genes required for the cloning procedure as well as four watermarks were incorporated~\cite{gibson_creation_2010}.
Reducing the genome from \syn~to \syna~(543{,}379~bp, 493 genes), while preserving the ancestral order of the retained genes, produced the minimal genome of an autonomously replicating organism.
\syna~in particular has drawn interest from laboratories worldwide seeking the essence of life, through studies of gene essentiality and metabolism~\cite{breuer_essential_2019}, three-dimensional organization~\cite{gilbert_generating_2021}, and cell division~\cite{pelletier_genetic_2021}.
Together these efforts enabled the first whole-cell model resolved in three-dimensional space across an entire cell cycle~\cite{thornburg_fundamental_2022, thornburg_bringing_2026}.

Bacterial genes are co-transcribed in operons~\cite{jacob_genetic_1961}, with multiple transcription start and termination sites acting across a single gene cluster, and downstream endo- and exoribonucleolytic processing further generates a spectrum of overlapping and truncated RNA isoforms (Fig.~\ref{fig:operon}a).
Resolving operons requires reading transcripts at full length: short-read Illumina sequencing quantifies transcript abundance robustly but fragments RNA and cannot show which genes share a transcript~\cite{stark_rna_2019}, whereas long-read sequencing recovers full-length molecules and so exposes operon co-transcription, processing isoforms, and antisense or intergenic transcription~\cite{yan_smrt-cappable-seq_2018, grunberger_nanopore_2022}.

Yet both cells have so far been defined chiefly at the level of genome design and protein essentiality~\cite{breuer_essential_2019}, leaving how their genes are organized and processed at the RNA level largely uncharted.
The two also differ in phenotype: \syna~divides more slowly~\cite{pelletier_genetic_2021}, packs a denser cytosol~\cite{gilbert_generating_2021}, and lacks persistent chromosome contacts, the last linked to its collapsed level of the nucleoid-associated protein HupA~\cite{gilbert_generating_2021}.
Three questions follow.
First, how is gene expression organized across a synthetic genome, from operon boundaries and transcription signals to downstream RNA processing?
Second, beyond the deletions themselves, what does this minimization, as a form of synthetic evolution, do to the transcription and translation program of the retained genes?
Third, how does that minimization contribute to the phenotypic differences between the two cells?

We therefore captured full-length transcripts with Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) and Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) in \syn~and ONT in \syna, using Illumina short reads as the standard for transcript quantification, and combined these with proteomics to build a genome-wide, operon-resolved map of transcription, RNA processing, and translation in both cells, and to ask how minimization reshaped it.
This atlas supplies the RNA-level foundation of gene co-expression and quantification that whole-cell modeling of the minimal cell has so far lacked.
